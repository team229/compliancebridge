Corporate Governance Best Practices for Indian Companies 2026

ComplianceBridge Advisory February 1, 2026 14 min read

Table of Contents

Corporate governance is the cornerstone of sustainable business growth and stakeholder trust in India. With evolving regulatory frameworks under the Companies Act, 2013, SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015, and RBI guidelines, companies must adopt robust governance practices. This guide provides a comprehensive overview of corporate governance best practices for Indian companies, covering board composition, independent directors, committee structures, secretarial audits, and compliance frameworks.

The importance of corporate governance in India has grown exponentially following high-profile corporate governance failures in recent decades. The Satyam scandal, the IL&FS crisis, and various other incidents have reinforced the need for stronger governance mechanisms. India's governance framework has evolved from a largely disclosure-based regime to one that emphasizes substantive compliance, board effectiveness, and stakeholder protection. For directors, company secretaries, and business owners, understanding these governance requirements is not merely a legal obligation but a strategic imperative for long-term business success.

The Corporate Governance Framework in India

The corporate governance framework in India is governed by a multi-layered regulatory structure. For listed companies, the primary regulations are the SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 (SEBI LODR), which prescribe comprehensive governance norms including board composition, committee formation, related party transactions, and disclosure requirements. For all companies, the Companies Act, 2013 provides the foundational governance framework, including provisions on directors, audit committees, independent directors, and secretarial audits.

The SEBI LODR regulations are applicable to all listed companies and have been progressively tightened over the years. The regulations require listed companies to comply with enhanced governance norms that go beyond the requirements of the Companies Act. For unlisted public companies and private companies, the Companies Act, 2013 provides the applicable governance framework, which is somewhat less stringent than the SEBI LODR requirements but still substantial.

For regulated entities such as NBFCs, banks, and insurance companies, the respective sectoral regulators (RBI, IRDAI) also prescribe additional governance norms that must be complied with alongside the general governance framework under the Companies Act and SEBI LODR. This multi-layered regulatory framework requires companies to maintain a comprehensive compliance approach that addresses all applicable governance requirements.

SEBI LODR Governance Requirements

The SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 prescribe several key governance requirements for listed companies. These include minimum board composition requirements, appointment of independent directors, constitution of board committees, maintenance of a compliance framework, and disclosure of material events and information.

Key SEBI LODR Governance Requirements:

  • Board Composition: At least one-third of the board must be independent directors (one-half if chairperson is an executive promoter)
  • Board Committees: Audit Committee, Nomination and Remuneration Committee, Stakeholders Relationship Committee, Risk Management Committee
  • Board Evaluation: Annual evaluation of board, committees, and individual directors
  • Related Party Transactions: Prior approval of the Audit Committee and shareholders for material RPTs
  • Corporate Governance Report: Quarterly compliance report on corporate governance requirements
  • Secretarial Audit: Annual secretarial audit by a company secretary in practice

Board Composition Requirements

The composition of the board of directors is a fundamental aspect of corporate governance. Under the Companies Act, 2013, the minimum number of directors for a public company is three, for a private company is two, and for an OPC is one. The maximum number of directors is fifteen, which can be increased by passing a special resolution.

For listed companies under SEBI LODR, the board must comprise at least six directors. At least one director must be a woman director. The board must have an appropriate mix of executive, non-executive, and independent directors to ensure effective governance and oversight. The board should also have a non-executive chairperson or an executive chairperson who is not a promoter.

The board must have an appropriate skills matrix that identifies the skills, experience, and expertise required for effective board functioning. The skills matrix should cover areas such as financial literacy, industry knowledge, governance expertise, risk management, digital technology, and sustainability. The Nomination and Remuneration Committee is responsible for assessing the skills required and recommending director appointments accordingly.

Independent Directors: Qualifications and Responsibilities

Independent directors play a crucial role in corporate governance by providing independent oversight and objective judgment. Under Section 149(6) of the Companies Act, 2013, an independent director is a director other than a managing director or whole-time director or nominee director who, in the opinion of the board, is a person of integrity and possesses relevant expertise and experience.

The criteria for qualifying as an independent director include: the person should not be a promoter or holding or subsidiary company or relative of a promoter, should not have a material pecuniary relationship with the company during the two preceding financial years, should not be or have been an employee or proprietor or partner of the statutory auditor, should not be or have been an employee of the company during the three preceding financial years, and should possess appropriate skills, experience, and knowledge in one or more fields of finance, law, management, sales, marketing, administration, research, corporate governance, technical operations, or other disciplines related to the company's business.

Independent directors must familiarize themselves with the company's affairs, business model, and governance framework. They must attend at least one board meeting per year and must participate in the annual board evaluation process. Independent directors are also required to undergo induction programs and continuing education programs as prescribed by the SEBI LODR regulations.

Board Evaluation Process

Board evaluation is a critical governance tool that helps assess the effectiveness of the board, its committees, and individual directors. Under Section 134(3)(p) of the Companies Act, 2013, the annual report must contain a statement on the evaluation of the board's performance. For listed companies, the SEBI LODR regulations prescribe a detailed framework for board evaluation.

The board evaluation process covers several key areas including board composition and structure, board dynamics and effectiveness, quality of information provided to directors, effectiveness of the chairperson, performance of individual directors, functioning of board committees, and the board's role in strategy formulation and oversight. The evaluation is typically conducted through questionnaires, discussions, and feedback mechanisms.

For listed companies, the evaluation of independent directors must be carried out by the entire board of directors (excluding the director being evaluated). The Nomination and Remuneration Committee is responsible for overseeing the evaluation process and recommending criteria for evaluation. The findings of the board evaluation must be disclosed in the corporate governance report filed with the stock exchanges.

Board Committees: Structure and Mandate

Board committees are essential governance mechanisms that enable focused attention on specific areas of governance and compliance. The Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI LODR regulations mandate the constitution of several key committees.

Mandatory Board Committees for Listed Companies:

  • Audit Committee: Minimum three directors, majority independent. Reviews financial statements, internal controls, auditor appointments, and related party transactions.
  • Nomination and Remuneration Committee: Minimum three directors, majority independent. Recommends director appointments, remuneration policy, and board evaluation criteria.
  • Stakeholders Relationship Committee: Presides over the redressal of grievances of shareholders, debenture holders, and other security holders.
  • Risk Management Committee: Reviews and recommends the risk management policy and framework. Comprises members with expertise in risk management.

Secretarial Audit: Requirements and Process

Under Section 204 of the Companies Act, 2013, certain companies are required to obtain a secretarial audit report from a company secretary in practice. The secretarial audit is an audit of compliance with applicable laws and regulations, and it covers the Companies Act, 2013, SEBI regulations, FEMA, labour laws, and other applicable statutes.

The companies required to obtain a secretarial audit include: listed companies, all public companies with paid-up capital of Rs 50 crore or more, and all public companies with turnover of Rs 250 crore or more. The secretarial audit must be conducted by a company secretary in practice who holds a valid certificate of practice from the Institute of Company Secretaries of India (ICSI).

The secretarial audit report (Form MR-3) must be appended to the board's report filed with the Registrar of Companies. The report covers compliance with the Companies Act and rules thereunder, SEBI regulations (for listed companies), FEMA regulations (if applicable), and other applicable laws. Any qualification or observation in the secretarial audit report must be reviewed by the board and addressed within the specified timelines.

Building a Comprehensive Compliance Framework

A robust corporate governance compliance framework is essential for ensuring that the company meets all its governance obligations in a timely and systematic manner. The framework should cover identification of applicable laws and regulations, appointment of compliance officers, implementation of compliance tracking systems, regular compliance monitoring, and reporting of compliance status to the board.

The compliance framework should include a compliance calendar that tracks all filing deadlines, meeting requirements, and disclosure obligations. The company secretary typically plays a central role in implementing and maintaining the compliance framework, including coordinating with legal counsel, auditors, and regulatory authorities as necessary.

For listed companies, the compliance framework must also address the requirements of the SEBI LODR regulations, including quarterly compliance reports, annual governance reports, and disclosure of material events. The framework should also include protocols for handling confidential information, insider trading prevention, and related party transaction management.

Penalties for Governance Non-Compliance

Non-compliance with corporate governance norms can result in severe penalties for companies and their directors. Under the Companies Act, 2013, penalties include monetary fines, officer liability, and prosecution in serious cases. Under the SEBI LODR regulations, penalties include monetary penalties, directions to comply, and listing restrictions.

  • Companies Act Penalties: Monetary fines up to Rs 3 lakh for the company and Rs 1 lakh per day for continuing default. Officer in default may face imprisonment up to 3 years or fine up to Rs 5 lakh, or both.
  • SEBI Penalties: Monetary penalties up to Rs 1 crore per violation. SEBI can also issue directions to comply, restrict capital raising, or initiate delisting proceedings.
  • Reputational Impact: Non-compliance can adversely impact the company's market reputation, investor confidence, and credit rating.
  • Director Liability: Independent directors may face personal liability for governance failures, particularly if they fail to exercise due diligence and care.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is corporate governance as per SEBI LODR?

Corporate governance as per SEBI LODR refers to the system of rules, practices, and processes by which companies are directed and controlled. It involves balancing the interests of stakeholders, ensuring accountability, transparency, and fairness in all aspects of company operations.

How many independent directors are required for listed companies?

Under SEBI LODR, listed companies must have at least one-third of the board as independent directors. If the chairperson is an executive promoter, at least half of the board must be independent directors.

What is a secretarial audit and who needs it?

A secretarial audit is an audit of compliance with applicable laws. Under Section 204 of the Companies Act, 2013, listed companies and public companies with paid-up capital of Rs 50 crore or more, or turnover of Rs 250 crore or more, must obtain a secretarial audit report.

What is the board evaluation process?

Board evaluation involves assessing the performance of the board, its committees, and individual directors. Under SEBI LODR, listed companies must carry out an annual evaluation covering attendance, participation, expertise, and contribution to strategy.

What are the penalties for non-compliance with governance norms?

Penalties include monetary fines up to Rs 3 lakh under the Companies Act, SEBI penalties up to Rs 1 crore per violation, delisting proceedings, officer liability including imprisonment, and adverse impact on market reputation.
